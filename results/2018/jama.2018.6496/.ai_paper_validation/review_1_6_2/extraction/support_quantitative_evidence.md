106   1      INTRODUCTION
107
108   Rapid and definitive airway management is an essential skill for all emergency
109   physicians. Orotracheal intubation is the most common means to obtain a definitive
110   airway, and is classically performed using an endotracheal tube with an intubating
111   sylet inserted into the tube for rigidity. The tube and stylet are passed under direct
112   vision. Using these methods, the majority of patients in the emergency department
113   can be successfully intubated, and therefore successfully oxygenated and ventilated.
114
115   The concept of first-pass success, that is, passing the endotracheal tube successfully
116   on the first intubation attempt, is paramount in emergency airway management. It
117   has been shown that for every attempt after the first, complications increase
118   dramatically.1 While emergency medicine has been improving airway management
119   and first pass success over the past several years, a large cross-sectional sample
120   demonstrated that first pass success remains approximately 85%.2 First pass
121   success is likely lower in the hands of less experienced operators, such as
122   emergency medicine residents in training. Therefore, there is substantial room for
123   improvement. A simple adjunct to endotracheal intubation, the gum elastic bougie
124   (GEB), may increase first pass success and decrease rates of intubation-associated
125   hypoxemia.
126
127   The GEB is a 60 or 70-centimeter stylet with an approximately 30-degree angle at its
128   tip. When used during an intubation attempt, the GEB is passed between the vocal
129   cords; then the endotracheal tube is passed over the GEB into the trachea. The GEB
130   essentially serves as a flexible guide into the trachea.
131
132   It can enable successful intubation in difficult airways due to its flexible material,
133   allowing the intubating provider to be able to direct its tip anteriorly through the
134   vocal cords. Proper placement of the GEB can be performed by direct visualization,
135   video assisted visualization, and also both the feeling of “clicks” as the GEB passes
136   over tracheal rings and a “hard stop” when the GEB comes into contact with a
137   mainstem bronchus at the level of the carina.3,4
138
139

146   case reports and case series describing the effectiveness of the GEB in these clinical
147   scenarios. 3,6-8 As the GEB became more popular, several larger series were
148   published supporting its use. One series of 2000 anesthesiology incident reports of
149   difficult intubations concluded that the most successful airway adjunct was the

206   1.2 Rationale for further investigation
207
208   Based on this review of the literature, there is evidence supporting GEB use as an
209   adjunct for difficult airways. However, because it is not always possible to
210   anticipate a difficult airway, or even semi-difficult airway, before an intubation
211   attempt begins, the bougie may improve the overall success of routine intubations
212   as well, especially for patients with any difficult airway characteristics.
213
214   However, while the GEB has significant face validity in its ability to improve
215   intubation success, a large multi-center study demonstrated that only 3.5% of first
216   attempts use the GEB.2 This speaks to the possibility that increasing the use of the
217   GEB, a simple, low-cost intervention, may improve first pass success and decrease
218   intubation-associated complications.
219
220   The practice in the Emergency Department at Hennepin County Medical Center
221   (HCMC), however, varies from nationwide practice in that the GEB is available for
222   every first intubation attempt. Based on the treating physicians preference, the GEB
223   may or may not be used on the first attempt. Thus, it is standard of care at HCMC to
224   use and not use the GEB on the first attempt. We have experienced faculty members,
225   many of whom are airway experts, who feel strongly on both sides, with some
226   stating that it should be used uniformly, and others saying that it should be reserved
227   for intubations that are not successful on the first attempt. Thus, there is a clinical
228   equipoise on whether to use or not use a GEB on the first attempt.

284   First pass success is defined as placement of the endotracheal tube (ETT) into the
285   trachea on the first attempt. An attempt begins when the laryngoscope enters the
286   mouth, and ends if either of the following occur:
287       1. the laryngoscope leaves the mouth, regardless of whether an attempt was
288          made to pass the endotracheal tube or bougie.

290          bougie), and switches to any other tube device, even if the laryngoscope
291          blade remains in the mouth.
292
293

295   successfully on the first attempt.

298   involving multiple modalities (physical exam, capnography, and chest x-ray, among
299   others).

303
304   1)     First pass success without hypoxemia. Hypoxemia is defined as a pulse
305          oximetry value (SpO2) less than 90% at any point during intubation, or a
306          drop of more than 10% from baseline if starting below 90%. The outcome of
307          hypoxemia will be recorded beginning when the first attempt begins and
308          ending one minute after inflation of the ETT cuff.
309
310          A patient will be considered to achieve this outcome if 1) they are intubated
311          successfully on the first attempt, and 2) do not experience hypoxemia on the
312          first attempt.
313

316          the time elapsed between the beginning of the intubation attempt to inflation
317          of the ETT cuff when the tube is in the trachea.
