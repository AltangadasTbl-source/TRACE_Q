Ancillary Study 1

Working Title: Cost Effectiveness Analysis of Metabolic Surgery for Obese Population with
Type 2 Diabetes

Submitted by: James Gallagher MD1,2, William Gourash PhD CRNP3, Mark Roberts MD MPP2,
Anita Courcoulas MD MPH3
1
  Department of Surgery, University of Pittsburgh
2
  Department of Health Policy and Management, Graduate School of Public Health, University of
Pittsburgh
3
  Division of MIS Bariatric & General Surgery, Department of Surgery, University of Pittsburgh

Possible Collaborators: All interested sites

Keywords: Bariatric surgery, metabolic surgery, cost-effectiveness analysis, type 2 diabetes,
class I obesity

Primary Endpoint: Cost-effectiveness of metabolic surgery in overweight persons with type 2
diabetes as defined by ICER < $100,000 per QALY (cost utility analysis)

Introduction and Background:
There is a growing body of evidence suggesting that surgical treatment of type 2 diabetes with
bariatric surgery is a safe and effective means of achieving durable improvement in glycemic
control in those with severe obesity.1,2 Thus, the concept of metabolic surgery is increasingly
recognized and incorporated into a treatment algorithms for diabetes management 3-5. Studies
primarily focus on class II and III obese population, while class I obesity largely lacks
randomized clinical data. Fortunately, many randomized control trials do include patients with
BMI less than 35 as a part of their larger study, particularly in the ARMMS-T2D Collaborative. 6-
10
   However, many questions remain regarding the proper role of surgery in overall diabetes care
in both obese and non-obese population. Economic evaluations are an important piece in this
overall puzzle.

A recent joint statement by international diabetes organizations suggested surgery should be
considered for patients with type 2 diabetes and class I obesity. 11 While there have been many
cost-effectiveness studies published demonstrating the cost-effectiveness (not cost savings) of
bariatric surgery in the morbidly obese population 12-15, these studies do not include class I
obesity. A study published using gastric banding was published this year demonstrating cost-
effectiveness with an incremental cost effectiveness ratio (ICER) of less than $50,000 per
quality adjusted life year (QALY). 16 However, this was a study performed in Australia and
converted to US dollars and only included gastric banding. There remains a lack of a robust cost
effectiveness analysis in bariatric surgery based on randomized clinical trial data comparing
surgery to intensive lifestyle and medical management for the class I obesity population in the
United States.

The ARMMS-T2D Collaborative has one of the largest populations of randomized patients with
class I obesity for study. By utilizing real world clinical data, a more accurate, applicable
economic model can be developed.

Specific Aim:
   x Evaluate cost-effectiveness of metabolic surgery compared to intensive lifestyle medical
       interventions for all patients with BMI 30-35 with type 2 diabetes using a state-transition
       (Markov) microsimulation model.

Methods and Study Design:
A cohort state-transition model (Markov model) was developed utilizing TreeAge Pro 2018
(TreeAge Software, Inc., Williamstown, MA) to compare cost-utility of two strategies: bariatric
surgery vs. incentive lifestyle modifications.
    x Cost effectiveness threshold will be set at $100,000/QALYs
           o Costs will be determined from the perspective of a third-party payer. No
               accounting for patient or caregiver time will be include.
           o Benefits will be reported using QALYs
    x Prior studies have utilized BMI reduction for utility changes, but this study will utilize
       quality of life (QoL) surveys captured in respective trials as this population starts at a
       lower BMI which may under represent benefit to patients if used exclusively
    x Background mortality will be estimated from US Third National Health and Nutritional
       Examination Survey17
    x Costs will be determined from national references, such as Medicare fee schedule,
       referencing utilization of patients in the included studies rather than site specific costs
       due to significant geographic variation in costs of care
    x Probabilistic sensitivity analyses will be performed to test assumptions made on model
       and impact on results

Timeline
Model development has already started and will continue to proceed without the required data.

be analyzed. Model development and debugging is expected to take 3 months and data
analysis will take 1-2 months after model completion.

participating sites.

as preoperative BMI, age, and comorbidity status. Longitudinal clinical information will be used
to estimate probabilities of developing certain outcomes, such as weight loss, comorbidity
changes, and adverse events. The model will use the probabilities to calculate expected values

Participants:
Eligible participants for ARMMS-T2D included those who were originally randomized in one of the four

18-65 years, (d) candidate for general anesthesia, (e) ability to perform exercise and other components of
the lifestyle intervention, and (f) negative urine pregnancy test at baseline (if applicable). T2D was
confirmed by requiring diabetes medication and/or having a diabetes diagnosis according to American
Diabetes Association criteria.1,2 Sex/gender data were collected by self-report with options of male,
female, other provided. Details of the original recruitment and randomization processes including the
methods of allocation concealment for each of the four original trials are available in these publications.3-
14
   None of the study teams were masked to group assignment.
All eligible participants were invited to continue long-term observation at their original site, and
voluntary informed written consent was obtained for participation in the pooled consortium study. The
study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at each site. A diagram depicting the
flow of patients from the original trials into the ARMMS-T2D follow-up study is shown in Figure 1,

Procedures:
Randomization occurred at each of the 4 sites independently and differed by site. At Cleveland Clinic,
participants were randomized to surgery (RYGB or SG) vs. medical/lifestyle therapy; at the Joslin
Diabetes Center/Brigham and Women’s Hospital participants chose which surgical procedure they
preferred and were then randomized to that procedure or to medical/lifestyle therapy; at the University of
Pittsburgh participants were randomized to RYGB, AGB, or medical/lifestyle therapy; and at University
of Washington/Kaiser Permanente Washington, to RYGB or medical/lifestyle. All surgical procedures
were performed laparoscopically. Those randomized to medical/lifestyle received individualized nutrition
counselling, instruction on exercise and self-monitoring of glucose, plus behavioral education and
counselling for at least 1 year after randomization. The protocols for delivery of the medical/lifestyle
intervention varied across sites and have been published with the original trials.3-14 After the first year, all
patients at University of Pittsburgh and Cleveland Clinic remained in a low level lifestyle intervention
including exercise and nutritional counseling monthly and participants at Joslin Diabetes Center/Brigham
and University of Washington/Kaiser Permanente Washington were encouraged to maintain participation
in supervised or unsupervised exercise, as well as to continue behavioral and nutritional counseling.

Statistical Supplementary Appendix
Statistical analysis:
Baseline participant characteristics were summarized as appropriate descriptive statistics for the
medical/lifestyle group, bariatric surgery group and each of the three surgical procedures. The analyses
followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. The primary outcome of change in HbA1c was assessed
using a linear mixed-effect model, which included group (medical/lifestyle vs. surgery), visit, their
interaction, site, and baseline HbA1c as fixed effects. The models also included a random intercept at the
participant level. The random intercept was assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution with a zero mean.
Least-square means were derived at each visit by group, and the group comparison at each visit was
performed by applying appropriate linear contrast to the model. The group comparison at year 7 was the
primary comparison. A secondary analysis was conducted where the binary group assignment was
replaced by a 4-category variable representing the three surgical procedures and the medical/lifestyle
intervention, allowing pairwise comparisons among the four groups with multiplicity adjustment.

Secondary outcomes for longitudinal continuous variables such as the percent changes of weight and
cardiometabolic risk factors (e.g., blood pressure and lipid parameters) were analyzed in the same manner
as the primary outcome. Each model controlled for site and the baseline value as the covariates. For
longitudinal binary outcomes such as diabetes remission, glycemic control and the use of medications for
diabetes, the generalized estimating equation approach was used, where the models included group, visit,
their interaction, site and baseline HbA1c as covariates. Least-square (LS) estimates were derived by visits
